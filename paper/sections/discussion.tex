\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
Almost all results are from the Tier-1 simulator, in which sensors are abstracted at the level of
landmark parts. Front-end errors (identity switches, clutter) are injected, but occlusion is
not modelled. A Tier-2 check in Gazebo uses kinematic sensor rigs, a ray-cast proxy for the
sonar and DAVE's simulated multibeam sonar images, but no vehicle dynamics; all results are
simulation. Six limitations shape the claims of this paper.
First, collaboration corrects a drifting AUV only when its solo drift is small. With metre-level
drift, each neighbour's frame absorbs the nearest cluster of matches rigidly, and the
trajectory does not bend. This needs anchors at both ends of the drift that are linked to the
team, and a front-end that recognizes at least some revisits: if it recognizes none, no robust
kernel restores the loop closures (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
Second, the gain is not uniform: in some runs the shared constraints make a drifting vehicle
worse. Part of this is unavoidable: in the worst such run even the oracle, with every robot's
data and true associations, is no better than the vehicle alone (Table~\ref{tab:drift}). The
rest is a gap between decentralized and centralized estimation: the constraints are correct and
their uncertainties consistent, but each neighbour's frame is a free variable and its landmarks
enter as fixed measurements, so the fused graph uses them less well than the oracle.
Third, ordering records by expected information did not beat a simple quality order. At
tens of bit/s the binding constraint is to deliver enough matchable records, not their
geometry.
Fourth, cycle consistency cannot check an alignment that closes no cycle. A weak alignment is
dropped when the other agent's estimate of the same pair contradicts it, but without such an
estimate it is used unchecked. The centralized baseline shows how an aliased alignment on sparse
data can then corrupt the whole team.
Multi-hop sharing without double counting is future work.
Fifth, intra-agent association. The Tier-2 estimator result uses ground-truth tracks. A tracker
that gates in the dead-reckoning frame fails once an underwater robot's drift approaches the
spacing of similar structures; the damage is mostly duplicate landmarks, that is, lost loop
closures, which no robust kernel repairs (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}). A filter over the robot's own estimate,
with the joint covariance of pose and landmarks and joint pairing of a keyframe's ambiguous
detections, recovers most of the gap, but it still fails occasionally for the robot whose sensor
gives sparse fixes: when its pose uncertainty is of the order of the pile spacing, a single
detection cannot separate a new pile from a mapped one. It also shares few landmarks with the
others, and an alignment built on four or five of them can be turned by half a revolution, which
moved the whole team until the reverse-estimate test of Sec.~\ref{sec:association}. Pairing over a window of
keyframes is future work. In simulation, a light LiDAR on that robot removes the failure
(Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}).
Sixth, the filter does not yet transfer to sonar images. The BlueROV2 that sees the fewest piles
has the Tarot's problem: under a pose uncertainty of the order of the pile spacing a single fix
cannot separate a mapped pile from a new one, and the duplicates it starts cost loop closures;
with ground-truth tracks the same images mostly suffice (Sec.~\ref{sec:experiments}). Pairing
fixes over a window of keyframes admitted more wrong matches than it saved; for the Tarot the
remedy was a sensor, and a second sonar is the analogue here. With a Ping360 on each BlueROV2
the filter reaches G1 in \sonarHeldTwoPEKFGOne{} held-out runs, against \sonarHeldTwoPGOne{}
with ground-truth tracks: the sensor closes part of the gap, and what remains is intra-agent
association. Tuning the filter for this sensor would need more development missions than the
twenty used here, so we report the gap rather than tune on the held-out missions.
