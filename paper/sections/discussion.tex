\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:discussion}
Almost all results are from the Tier-1 simulator, in which sensors are abstracted at the level of
landmark parts. Front-end errors (identity switches, clutter) are injected, but occlusion is
not modelled. A Tier-2 check in Gazebo uses kinematic sensor rigs and a ray-cast proxy for the
sonar, not a physics-based sonar or vehicle dynamics; all results are simulation. Five
limitations shape the claims of this paper.
First, collaboration corrects a drifting AUV only when its solo drift is small. With metre-level
drift, each neighbour's frame absorbs the nearest cluster of matches rigidly, and the
trajectory does not bend. This needs anchors at both ends of the drift that are linked to the
team.
Second, the gain is not uniform: in a few runs the shared constraints make a drifting vehicle
slightly worse. Holding its heading bias at the local estimate removed most such cases.
Third, ordering records by expected information did not beat a simple quality order. At
tens of bit/s the binding constraint is to deliver enough matchable records, not their
geometry.
Fourth, cycle consistency cannot check an alignment that closes no cycle. The centralized
baseline shows how an aliased alignment on sparse data can then corrupt the whole team.
Multi-hop sharing without double counting is future work.
Fifth, intra-agent association. The Tier-2 result uses ground-truth tracks. A tracker that
gates in the dead-reckoning frame fails once an underwater robot's drift approaches the spacing of
similar structures, and its errors are persistent, unlike the random identity switches injected in
Tier 1, which the robust kernel absorbs. Association against the agent's own estimate is future
work, and the Tier-1 error model may understate the danger.
